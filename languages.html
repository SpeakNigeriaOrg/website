Nigeria's languages

One country, hundreds of languages.

Nigeria is one of the most linguistically rich places on earth — home to more than 500 languages, spread across several major families. We can't build for all of them at once, but we can be thoughtful about how we grow. Here's the landscape we're working within.

The big picture

Three major language families.

Nigeria's languages don't all descend from one root. They belong to several distinct families that developed separately over thousands of years — which means they can differ enormously in their sounds, tones, and grammar.

Niger-Congo

The largest family

The biggest language family in Africa, and the one most Nigerian languages belong to. It stretches across most of the south and centre of the country.

In Nigeria, it includes: Yorùbá, Igbo, Edo (Bìnì), Efik, Ibibio, Tiv, Idoma, Igala, Nupe, Urhobo, and Fulfulde, among many others. Ijaw (Izon) sits on one of the family's more distinct branches.

Afroasiatic

Reaching across a continent

A family that spans North Africa, the Horn, and the Middle East. In Nigeria, its Chadic branch is especially important.

In Nigeria, it includes: Hausa — the most widely spoken language in the country — along with many smaller Chadic languages across the north.

Nilo-Saharan

The north-east

A family centred on the Nile and the Lake Chad basin, present in Nigeria's north-eastern corner.

In Nigeria, it includes: Kanuri, the language of the historic Kanem–Bornu Empire, and related languages of the Borno region.

A note on humility: linguists continue to debate exactly how these families branch and where some languages belong. We hold these groupings lightly and defer to native speakers and scholars of each language.

Where we fit

Starting close to home, building outward.

We began with Yorùbá, and we're building toward Igbo — both Niger-Congo languages, and both close to the communities we started with. From there, we want to reach across families over time.

That's harder than it sounds, and it's part of why we work in the open. A learning tool built for Yorùbá can't simply be copied over to Hausa (Afroasiatic) or Kanuri (Nilo-Saharan) — the sounds and structures are genuinely different. Getting each language right takes speakers, teachers, and recordings from that language's own community. The more communities take part, the further we can go.
